# Design principles

These principles reflect how people think, feel, and interact with the world. They guide the Human Interface Guidelines, but there is no single right application: use them to weigh competing priorities and make design decisions.

| Principle | Core directive |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Make something meaningful. |
| **Agency** | Let people do things their own way. |
| **Responsibility** | Act in people’s best interest. |
| **Familiarity** | Build on what people know. |
| **Flexibility** | Adapt to diverse contexts and needs. |
| **Simplicity** | Be clear and direct. |
| **Craft** | Care about every detail. |
| **Delight** | Make it human. |

## Purpose

Create value by repeatedly asking what the product is for and whether the design serves it. Keep focused on the most important features and how people want to use them. Investigate existing solutions, avoid re-creating them, and define what sets the product apart.

## Agency

Stay out of the way: often the best way to help is to bring people directly to the task or content, with the interface unobtrusive and present when needed. Let people explore without locking them into flows or modes; when a guided flow is necessary, make it easy to skip or escape. Build forgiveness into the design so people can reverse actions or return to a previous state without losing time or work.

## Responsibility

Make intentions clear from the first interaction. Give a rationale for permissions and explain what data you collect and how you use it. Collect only what the product needs, handle it carefully, anticipate misuse or harm, and protect against abuse and unintended consequences.

## Familiarity

Draw on real-world and software concepts people know. Keep visual and interaction behavior consistent so people learn quickly and can predict new interactions. Provide clear feedback about available controls and content changes, using system patterns for alerts and choices.

## Flexibility

Design for diverse experiences, perspectives, and needs; treat accessibility as a priority from the start. Preserve context across platforms and configurations with consistent, predictable content and controls and natural transitions. Consider a variety of input methods, including voice, touch, and keyboard, and try to support as many devices, types of interaction, and perspectives as possible. Approach every platform intentionally: give each supported platform the same level of care so the product feels polished and at home wherever it runs.

## Simplicity

Include what is necessary; simplicity is not minimalism. Be concise and choose exactly the words needed for a concept or control. Establish hierarchy so form and function are apparent: prioritize recognizable controls and a consistent structure so people understand where they are and what comes next.

## Craft

Be deliberate with every element, including visuals, animation, wording, and audio; strive for stunning visuals, smooth animation, precise wording, and thoughtful audio. Prototype early, experiment, discard what fails, refine each feature to a high bar, and test in real-world settings for durability, reliability, and performance. After shipping, keep the interface current with platform capabilities and patterns and maintain a high quality bar.

## Delight

Choose the emotion appropriate to the product and let it shape the experience. From a button press to an error message, consider whether each interaction is an opportunity to express character. Delight should not obstruct the core task; balance practical refinement and whimsy. It emerges from the whole experience: freedom to act, safety to explore, familiar metaphors, and flexible transitions, all delivered with intent, focus, and care.

Platform considerations: applies across iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS, and watchOS.
