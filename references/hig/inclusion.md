# Inclusion

Inclusive apps and games put people first through respectful communication and content and functionality everyone can access and understand. Review words, images, and experiences iteratively. Examine assumptions about how others think and feel, and stay open to evolving knowledge.

## Inclusive by design

Investigate people’s goals and perspectives so content resonates. Empathy can reveal that a word or image is incomprehensible or carries an unintended meaning. Perspectives are distinct and dynamic intersections of shared human characteristics and experiences, including age; gender and gender identity; race and ethnicity; sexuality; physical and cognitive attributes; permanent, temporary, and situational disabilities; language and culture; religion; education; political or philosophical opinions; and social and economic context.

Inclusion is more than avoiding offense: an inoffensive experience is not necessarily inclusive. Focus on creating a welcoming experience everyone can enjoy.

## Welcoming language

Use clear, direct, respectful copy and review its tone from different perspectives; an academic tone, for example, can suggest that only highly educated people are welcome. Typically address people as **you** and **your** rather than the distant **user** or **player**. Consider reserving **we** and **our** for the software or company, since implying a personal relationship can feel insulting or condescending.

Define specialized or technical terms before using them and make definitions easy to find. Even when people know a term’s definition, plain language is easier to read and translate. Replace culture-specific colloquialisms with plain language; some also have exclusionary origins (for example, “peanut gallery” and “grandfathered in”). Consider humor carefully: it is subjective, difficult to translate, and can confuse, irritate, or insult.

## Being approachable

An approachable app does not require particular skills or knowledge at the outset and provides a path to deeper understanding. Use a clear, straightforward interface appropriate to the platform. Consider onboarding that teaches step by step while letting experienced people skip to the content they want.

## Gender identity

Avoid unnecessary gender references. Prefer gender-neutral nouns and plural constructions (for example, “Subscribers can post recipes…” instead of “a subscriber…his or her…”), which also localize better in languages with gendered pronouns. For avatars, emoji, glyphs, and game characters, avoid unnecessary gendering and prefer giving people customization tools. For a generic person, use nongendered imagery; SF Symbols includes nongendered **person.crop.circle**, **person.3.fill**, and **figure.wave** symbols.

Most apps do not need gender. If you require gender information, such as for health or legal reasons, consider **nonbinary**, **self-identify**, and **decline to state**; you could also let people specify their pronouns so you can address them properly when necessary.

## People, settings, and stereotypes

In copy and images, portray a range of human characteristics and activities, such as racial backgrounds, body types, ages, and physical capabilities. Avoid stereotypes such as only male doctors, female nurses, or demographic mappings of heroes and villains. Review settings and objects too: high affluence may fit one scenario but make another feel out of touch; when appropriate, show places, homes, activities, and items familiar and relatable to most people.

Audit unconscious assumptions. A narrow family model (woman, man, biological children) excludes other families. Security questions should rely on experiences people are likely to share, not culture- or capability-specific events: “What was the make of your first car?” can become “What’s your favorite activity?”

## Accessibility

Support VoiceOver, Display Accommodations, closed captioning, Switch Control, Speak Screen, and other system features. Do not assume a disability prevents someone from wanting the experience. Disabilities are a spectrum (for example, low vision through blindness, including color blindness, light sensitivity, and peripheral vision loss), and temporary or situational disabilities can affect anyone (such as infection-related hearing loss or a noisy train).

Include people with disabilities in representations and avoid language or imagery that uses disability as a negative quality. Take a people-first approach when writing about disability, while checking how a specific person or community self-identifies. Prefer familiar, consistent interactions and make content perceivable through sight, hearing, or touch.

## Languages and localization

Prepare for internationalization: support languages and regions beyond your own, including locale-specific date, time, and money formatting, then provide translated text and resources. Plain language, gender-neutral copy, diverse representation, and avoidance of stereotypes and culture-specific content make localization easier. SF Symbols provides language-specific glyphs and symbols that work in left-to-right and right-to-left contexts.

Color can carry different meanings by locale (white can signify death or grief in some places and purity or peace in others). Research each locale and ensure color communicates the intended meaning in every version.

Platform considerations: no additional considerations for iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS, or watchOS.
